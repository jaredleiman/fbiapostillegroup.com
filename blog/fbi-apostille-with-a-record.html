FBI Apostille When Your Report Shows a Record

The apostille does not read your report

An apostille answers one narrow question: is this document genuinely what it claims to be, signed by the official whose name it carries? The U.S. Department of State authenticates the FBI's issuance of your Identity History Summary. It does not review the contents, weigh the entries, or adjust anything it finds. A report with arrests or convictions on it is apostilled on exactly the same terms as a report with none, because the apostille is about the document, not about you.

Nothing is hidden, and nothing is cleaned

Two hopes follow some applicants into this process, and both are misplaced. The apostille does not soften, seal, or reframe a record for the receiving country; the foreign authority reads the same summary the FBI issued. And it is not a step toward clearing anything. Expungement and sealing are court processes in the jurisdiction that owns the record. Authentication is performed on a finished document; it changes the document's standing abroad, not its contents.

Who decides what your record means

The receiving authority does, under its own law. Countries write their own rules about which offenses bar which visas: South Korea's teaching visa treats convictions as disqualifying under its immigration rules, Spain asks for the certificate and evaluates the whole file, and other countries set their own lines. Those decisions belong to consulates and immigration officers, and where the answer is unclear, to an immigration attorney who practices in that system. A document service cannot predict them, and any service that promises a record will "be fine" after an apostille is promising something no apostille does.

Errors are a different problem, with a different door

Sometimes the issue is not the record but the record-keeping: a charge shown without its dismissal, an entry that belongs to someone else, a disposition the court never reported. Correcting the summary runs through the FBI's challenge process or the state repository that contributed the entry, before authentication, not after. Apostilling a report you are about to have corrected authenticates a version that is about to be replaced, and freshness windows at the destination may not leave time to do it twice.

The practical order

Read your own summary before a consulate does. If it is accurate, the apostille step is mechanical: the document goes to the Department of State as issued, and the visa file turns on the destination's rules, not on the authentication. If it is inaccurate, fix the record first, through the channel that owns it. Either way, the apostille is the last step on the document, and it is the same step for every report the FBI issues.

Frequently Asked Questions: FBI Apostille With a Record

Can the Department of State refuse to apostille my report because of what is on it?

No. Authentication concerns the document's origin, that the FBI issued it, not its contents.

Will an apostille help my chances if a country is strict about records?

It neither helps nor hurts. It is a required formality for the document; the eligibility decision is the receiving authority's, made on the record itself.

My report shows an arrest that was dismissed, with no outcome listed. What should I do?

That is a record-completeness issue. The outcome is supplied by the court or repository that reported the arrest, and correcting it happens through the FBI's challenge process or that repository, before you apostille, so the authenticated version is the final one.
