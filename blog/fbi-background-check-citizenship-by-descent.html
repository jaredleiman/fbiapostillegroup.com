Foreign Citizenship by Descent: Why Your FBI Background Check Is Part of the Application

FBI Apostille GroupAugust 20265 min read

Millions of Americans qualify for a second citizenship through a parent, grandparent, or even great-grandparent, and citizenship by descent has become one of the most popular paths to a second passport. What surprises many applicants is that proving your ancestry isn't always enough. Many countries also want to know who you are today, and that's where the FBI background check comes in.

What Citizenship by Descent Actually Is

Citizenship by descent (sometimes called citizenship by ancestry or jus sanguinis, "right of blood") means a country recognizes you as a citizen because of your family line, not because you were born there or moved there. Countries with well-known descent programs include Italy, Ireland, Poland, Germany, Hungary, Slovakia, and Portugal, among many others. Each has its own rules about how many generations back you can claim and what breaks the chain, like an ancestor naturalizing elsewhere before the next generation was born.

For Americans, the appeal is obvious: an EU passport through an Italian great-grandparent or a Polish grandmother opens the right to live, work, and study across Europe, without giving up U.S. citizenship in most cases.

Where the FBI Background Check Fits

While the core of a descent application is genealogical (birth, marriage, and death records tracing your line), a number of countries also require a criminal record check on the applicant. The reasoning is simple: they're granting you full citizenship, and they want confirmation that you don't have a disqualifying criminal history.

For an American applicant, the recognized document for this is the FBI Identity History Summary, the federal-level background check drawn from the FBI's national database. State-level police checks generally don't satisfy the requirement, because they only cover one state; the FBI check covers your entire U.S. record.

Countries and Situations Where This Comes Up

Requirements vary by country and can vary by consulate, but the background check requirement commonly appears in:

  • Descent and ancestry applications where the country screens adult applicants for criminal history
  • Cases where the descent application is processed in-country rather than through a consulate
  • Related steps in the process, such as registering residency in the ancestral country while your citizenship claim is processed

Even when a country's descent program doesn't strictly require a background check, applicants often need one anyway for the residence permit they hold while living there during processing.

Timing Matters More Than People Expect

Two timing realities trip up descent applicants:

Validity Windows

Many countries treat background checks as fresh evidence, valid only for 3 to 6 months from issuance. Order it too early in a multi-year genealogical document hunt and it will expire before you file. The background check is usually one of the last documents you should obtain, timed to your submission date.

Consular Appointment Backlogs

Popular descent programs (Italy especially) have long waits for consular appointments. Knowing your appointment date lets you time the FBI check so it's valid on the day you appear.

The Bottom Line

If you're pursuing citizenship by descent, put the FBI background check on your document checklist early, but obtain it late. Check your target country's specific requirements, note the validity window, and time it to your filing date.
