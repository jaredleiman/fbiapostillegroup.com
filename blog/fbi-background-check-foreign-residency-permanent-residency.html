Moving Abroad? Why Nearly Every Foreign Residency Application Asks for Your FBI Background Check

FBI Apostille GroupAugust 20265 min read

Whether you're retiring to Portugal, taking a remote job to Mexico, joining a spouse in Germany, or relocating for work in Japan, one document shows up on almost every residency application an American files: a criminal background check. And for U.S. citizens, the version foreign governments want is the FBI Identity History Summary, the federal check covering your entire U.S. record.

Residency vs. Permanent Residency: A Quick Distinction

Temporary residency is the entry point in most countries: a renewable permit tied to a purpose, such as retirement income, remote work, employment, study, or family reunification. Common examples Americans pursue include retirement visas, digital nomad visas, and spousal or family permits.

Permanent residency typically comes later, after a qualifying number of years as a temporary resident. It removes the renewal treadmill, usually decouples your status from a specific job or income source, and in many countries starts the clock toward citizenship eligibility.

Both stages frequently require a background check, and some countries ask for a fresh one at each renewal or upgrade.

Why Foreign Governments Want the FBI Check Specifically

Immigration authorities want to screen applicants for criminal history before granting the right to live in their country. For Americans, they almost always specify a national-level check rather than a state or local one, for a simple reason: a state police check from Indiana says nothing about what may be on record in Florida or California. The FBI Identity History Summary is the single document that covers your full U.S. history, which is why it's the standard requirement in residency applications worldwide.

Common residency scenarios where it's required:

  • Retirement visas: countries with popular retirement pathways screen applicants' criminal history as a standard condition
  • Digital nomad and remote work visas: most of the newer remote-work visa programs include a clean-record requirement
  • Family reunification: joining a foreign spouse or family member usually triggers a background check on the incoming American
  • Employment-based permits: many countries require one before issuing or converting a work-linked residence permit
  • Permanent residency upgrades: moving from temporary to permanent status often requires a new, current check even if you provided one years earlier

The Validity Window Problem

The single biggest mistake Americans make with residency background checks is timing. Most countries only accept a background check issued within the last 3 to 6 months. People understandably want to check items off their list early, then discover at filing time that their check has expired and must be redone.

Rule of Thumb

Figure out your actual submission or appointment date first, then obtain the FBI check as one of the final steps, so it's comfortably inside the validity window when your application is reviewed.

Every Country Reads "Recent" Differently

Beyond validity windows, countries differ on details: whether the check must cover just the U.S. or every country you've lived in during the past 5 or 10 years, whether a certified translation is needed, and whether renewals require a new check. Reading your specific country's current checklist, ideally from the consulate handling your application, prevents expensive surprises.

The Bottom Line

If a move abroad is on your horizon, expect the FBI background check to be part of your residency application, and plan its timing around your filing date rather than your excitement level.
